    01 / What it actually does

    Three jobs. That is the whole product.

    Named for two old ideas that fit it perfectly: Indra, the sky-watcher with a thousand eyes, and Agni, fire aimed exactly where it is needed. Seeing, and answering, working as one.

    Job one / the eye
    It combines what everything sees into one picture
    Five sensors might each spot the same drone and report it separately. The software figures out that is one drone, not five, and gives everyone the same clean view of what is actually out there.
    Job two / the fire
    It picks the cheapest weapon that will work
    A slow cheap drone does not need a $150,000 missile. A jammer or a gun handles it. A fast cruise missile does need the expensive shot. The software makes that call for every threat, in real time, faster than a person can. That is the whole business: you stop wasting expensive missiles on cheap targets.
    Job three / the net
    It keeps working when parts get destroyed
    Because it runs across every position instead of from one control center, knocking a few out does not break it. The surviving positions automatically take over the threats the dead ones were handling.

    03 / Why it matters

    Cheap drones are winning wars because defenders answer them with expensive missiles.

    • We do not build weapons
      The base already owns the sensors and the weapons. We bring software that makes them work together and choose wisely. Nothing to manufacture, nothing that explodes.
    • It plugs into what they already have
      The Army asked for software that snaps onto its open systems. Indragni is exactly that layer: it rides on top of any sensor or weapon instead of locking them to one vendor.
    • It proves itself before a shot is fired
      The whole thing can be shown in a simulation like the one above, so a small team can win its first contract without ever touching live hardware.
